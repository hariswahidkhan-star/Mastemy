## Learning outcomes

1. Define epidemiology and describe its role in public health
2. Distinguish measures of disease frequency: incidence and prevalence
3. Compare major study designs and their strengths and limitations
4. Interpret basic measures of association such as risk ratios conceptually
5. Explain concepts of bias, confounding and causation
6. Summarise principles of surveillance, screening and outbreak investigation

## What this course assesses / does not assess

- Assesses: knowledge and applied reasoning on every in-scope outcome, using single-answer MCQ and multiple-answer selection only.
- Does not assess: Assesses knowledge and applied reasoning through selection items; does not assess hands-on performance.

## Sample items (original, draft, unreviewed)

**MST-2134-Q0001** (single-answer, Select ONE) The number of NEW cases of a disease arising in a population over a defined period is the:

- A. Incidence **(key)**  
  _Rationale:_ Correct: incidence counts new cases over a time period.
- B. Prevalence  
  _Rationale:_ Prevalence counts existing (new plus pre-existing) cases at a point or period.
- C. Mortality ratio  
  _Rationale:_ That concerns deaths, not new cases of disease.
- D. Attack rate denominator  
  _Rationale:_ This is not the definition of new-case frequency.

**MST-2134-Q0002** (multiple-answer, Select TWO) Which TWO are strengths of a prospective cohort study? (Select TWO.)

- A. It can measure incidence directly **(key)**  
  _Rationale:_ Correct: following people over time yields incidence.
- B. Temporal sequence of exposure before outcome is clearer **(key)**  
  _Rationale:_ Correct: exposure is recorded before the outcome occurs.
- C. It is the fastest and cheapest design for rare diseases  
  _Rationale:_ Case-control is usually better for rare diseases; cohorts are slow and costly.
- D. It eliminates all confounding automatically  
  _Rationale:_ Cohorts do not remove confounding by design.
- E. It requires no follow-up  
  _Rationale:_ Cohort studies require follow-up over time.

**MST-2134-Q0003** (single-answer, Select ONE) A factor that is associated with both the exposure and the outcome, and can distort an apparent association, is called a:

- A. Confounder **(key)**  
  _Rationale:_ Correct: a confounder is linked to both exposure and outcome and can distort the association.
- B. Mediator on the causal path only  
  _Rationale:_ A mediator lies on the causal pathway and is treated differently from a confounder.
- C. Random error  
  _Rationale:_ Random error is chance variation, not a systematic third-variable distortion.
- D. Outcome variable  
  _Rationale:_ The outcome is what is being measured, not the distorting factor.
