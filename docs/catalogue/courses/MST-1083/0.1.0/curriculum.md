## Sample items (original, draft, unreviewed)

**MST-1083-Q0001** (single-answer, Select ONE) Research consistently shows that, compared with unstructured interviews, structured interviews:

- A. Predict job performance less reliably  
  _Rationale:_ The opposite is true; structure improves predictive validity.
- B. Predict job performance more reliably and reduce bias **(key)**  
  _Rationale:_ Correct: standardised questions and scoring raise validity and fairness.
- C. Are only useful for junior roles  
  _Rationale:_ Structure benefits selection at all levels.
- D. Remove the need for any scoring guide  
  _Rationale:_ A scoring guide is a core part of what makes an interview structured.

**MST-1083-Q0002** (single-answer, Select ONE) The fairest basis for shortlisting candidates is to:

- A. Pick the CVs that feel most impressive  
  _Rationale:_ Gut feel invites bias and is hard to defend.
- B. Score every application against the agreed selection criteria **(key)**  
  _Rationale:_ Correct: consistent scoring against defined criteria is fair and defensible.
- C. Prefer candidates from familiar employers  
  _Rationale:_ Prestige bias excludes capable people and is not job-relevant.
- D. Shortlist whoever applied first  
  _Rationale:_ Order of application says nothing about suitability.

**MST-1083-Q0003** (multiple-answer, Select TWO) Which TWO are common biases a selection process should guard against? (Select TWO)

- A. Halo effect, where one strong trait colours the whole judgement **(key)**  
  _Rationale:_ Correct: the halo effect distorts overall scoring from a single impression.
- B. Similarity bias, favouring candidates who resemble the interviewer **(key)**  
  _Rationale:_ Correct: affinity for people like us undermines merit-based selection.
- C. Using a structured scoring rubric  
  _Rationale:_ A rubric reduces bias; it is not itself a bias.
- D. Comparing candidates against the job criteria  
  _Rationale:_ Criteria-based comparison is good practice, not a bias.
