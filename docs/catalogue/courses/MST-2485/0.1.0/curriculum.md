## Learning outcomes

1. Navigate the structure and main sections of a published set of financial statements
2. Interpret the income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement and how they reconcile
3. Use the notes and accounting policies to understand what the numbers represent
4. Apply common ratios to assess profitability, liquidity, efficiency and solvency
5. Identify signs that warrant further questions or caution
6. Explain findings from a set of statements to a non-specialist audience

## What this course assesses / does not assess

- Assesses: knowledge and applied reasoning on every in-scope outcome, using single-answer MCQ and multiple-answer selection only.
- Does not assess: Assesses knowledge and applied reasoning through selection items; does not assess hands-on performance.

## Sample items (original, draft, unreviewed)

**MST-2485-Q0001** (single-answer, Select ONE) Where would you look first to understand how a company recognises revenue?

- A. The accounting policies within the notes to the financial statements **(key)**  
  _Rationale:_ Correct: recognition policies are disclosed in the notes.
- B. The front-cover marketing summary  
  _Rationale:_ Marketing material is not the authoritative policy source.
- C. The auditor's fee note only  
  _Rationale:_ Audit fees do not describe revenue recognition.
- D. The share price chart  
  _Rationale:_ Share price is unrelated to recognition policy.

**MST-2485-Q0002** (multiple-answer, Select TWO) Which TWO belong in the operating section of the cash flow statement? (Select TWO.)

- A. Cash received from customers **(key)**  
  _Rationale:_ Correct: customer receipts are operating cash flow.
- B. Cash paid to suppliers and employees **(key)**  
  _Rationale:_ Correct: supplier and payroll payments are operating cash flow.
- C. Cash paid to buy a new factory  
  _Rationale:_ That is investing cash flow.
- D. Proceeds from issuing new shares  
  _Rationale:_ That is financing cash flow.

**MST-2485-Q0003** (single-answer, Select ONE) Net profit rose but operating cash flow fell sharply. What is the most reasonable concern to raise?

- A. Profit may be supported by rising receivables or inventory rather than cash collection **(key)**  
  _Rationale:_ Correct: a profit/cash gap often signals working-capital build-up or aggressive recognition.
- B. The company must be committing fraud  
  _Rationale:_ A gap warrants questions, not an unfounded accusation.
- C. Nothing, cash flow is irrelevant if profit rises  
  _Rationale:_ Cash flow is highly relevant to viability.
- D. The statements must be arithmetically wrong  
  _Rationale:_ The two can legitimately diverge.
